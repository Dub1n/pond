# Indoor Temperate Bog / Aquatic Plant Collection

## Concept

A low-maintenance indoor temperate wetland/paludarium using one large watertight container divided into connected hydrological zones.
The system should provide a continuous transition from: **damp gravelly bog → saturated bog → shallow marginal shelf → open water**
All zones share the same underlying water reservoir. Substrate height and composition, rather than separate sealed containers, create the different growing conditions.
The system should remain relatively cool rather than being operated as a warm tropical aquarium.

Large rocks, slate, inert blocks or perforated/mesh dividers can be used to retain the different substrate heights while still allowing water to move between zones.
The taller plants should generally occupy the back of the system so that they do not excessively shade the submerged plants.

## Plants

### Oxygenators / open-water plants

| Plant | Position |
| --- | --- |
| **Ranunculus aquatilis** — Common Water Crowfoot | Open water; submerged |
| **Fontinalis antipyretica** — Willow Moss | Open water; attached to submerged rock/wood |
| **Callitriche stagnalis** — Common Water Starwort | Open water; submerged with growth allowed to reach the surface |

### Fontinalis

Attach to a submerged rock, stone or piece of wood rather than burying it in substrate.
Place where there is gentle water movement.

### Callitriche

Allow stems to grow upward through the water and form floating/surface growth.

### Ranunculus

Give it a reasonably open, well-lit portion of the water chamber rather than allowing the marginal plants to shade it heavily.
The open-water section should be approximately **20–35 cm deep** where practical.

| Plant | Preferred zone |

## Substrate and structure

Avoid filling the whole system with rich soil.
Use inert bulk material to create most of the landscape:

- washed gravel;
- coarse aggregate;
- lava rock;
- LECA;
- stone/slate;
- inert blocks or overturned aquatic baskets where hidden volume is required.

Use actual soil only in the planted root zones.
This reduces nutrient release into the water and helps prevent excessive algae.

### Lower bog and marginal substrate

Use a predominantly mineral substrate such as:

- aquatic loam or heavy low-organic topsoil;
- optional coarse sand;
- washed gravel cap.

A typical planted pocket can contain roughly **8–15 cm of suitable loam**, depending on the plant and available depth.
Cap exposed underwater soil with approximately **2–4 cm of washed gravel** to prevent it dispersing into the water.
Avoid ordinary multipurpose compost in submerged areas.

### Upper bog

Use a somewhat more freely structured mixture, approximately:

- 2 parts loam;
- 1 part coarse sand/grit;
- 1 part fine gravel.

The lower portion should remain in hydraulic contact with the common reservoir so that the substrate remains continuously moist.

## Water system

### Shared reservoir

The bog, marginal shelf and open-water area should all communicate with a common body of water.
The upper zones should therefore remain moist through capillary movement and contact with the reservoir rather than requiring independent watering.

### Circulation

Use a small, low-power adjustable pump.
Target approximately **100–300 L/h before throttling**.
Actual flow should be kept gentle.
A useful arrangement is:

```text
open water
    ↓
small pump
    ↓
hose hidden behind rocks
    ↓
upper gravel / bog area
    ↓
slow seep through the landscape
    ↓
open water
```

This provides:

- gentle oxygenation;
- movement through the root zones;
- reduced stagnation;
- suitable circulation around the Willow Moss;
- a naturally wetter-to-drier gradient.

Fit a coarse sponge or intake guard to the pump to prevent roots and debris entering it.
A large aquarium-style biological/mechanical filter is not required unless later experience shows that the water quality needs one.

## Water level

Maintain a **constant reservoir level**.

The system should include:

- permanently submerged open water;
- shallow-water marginal areas;
- saturated substrate;
- upper damp substrate above the nominal waterline.

Marking the intended operating waterline on the inside of the container will make topping up easier.
If possible, include a low-level drain, bulkhead or easily accessible siphon point at the open-water end.
This makes partial water changes much simpler.

## Lighting

Use a broad **full-spectrum horticultural LED** covering most or all of the planting area.
The fixture should ideally be height-adjustable because the collection contains both low submerged plants and tall marginals such as Cyperus and Iris.
Start with approximately **10–12 hours of light per day**.
Watch the submerged section in particular for shading from the larger emergent plants.
Trim or reposition tall growth where necessary to preserve sufficient light for the oxygenators.

## Temperature

Treat the system as a **cool-temperate wetland**, not a tropical aquarium.

Avoid:

- radiators;
- hot windowsills;
- aquarium heaters;
- prolonged warm stagnant water.

A useful target is approximately:

- **winter: 10–17 °C where practical**
- **active growing period: 16–22 °C**

Ordinary indoor temperatures are acceptable, but keeping the system somewhat cooler in winter should better accommodate the seasonal behaviour of the temperate plants.
Avoid prolonged water temperatures substantially above approximately **22 °C** where possible.

## Initial planting

When the plants arrive:

1. Inspect and rinse excessive loose nursery substrate from the roots where appropriate.
2. Remove obviously dead or damaged material.
3. Build the inert landscape and establish the intended waterline before planting.
4. Add the individual loam/root pockets.
5. Plant the upper and marginal plants at their intended crown height.
6. Gravel-cap submerged or erosion-prone soil.
7. Fill slowly to avoid disturbing the substrate.
8. Attach Willow Moss to submerged hardscape.
9. Introduce the remaining oxygenators to the open-water zone.
10. Start the circulation pump at low flow.
11. Begin with the lighting schedule at approximately 10 hours/day and adjust according to plant response and algae growth.

New 7 cm bare-root plants should initially be given enough space that their crowns are not immediately smothered by neighbouring plants.

## Establishment period

During the first few weeks:

- keep the water level particularly consistent;
- expect some old foliage to die back after transplanting;
- remove decaying leaves before they accumulate underwater;
- watch for plants being planted too deeply;
- reduce lighting duration if algae increases rapidly;
- avoid heavy fertilisation immediately after planting.

The system may initially release nutrients and become somewhat cloudy while the substrates and plants settle.

## Fertilising

Do not routinely dose the whole water column with general liquid plant fertiliser. Instead, feed primarily through the root zones.
Use aquatic/root fertiliser tablets or another controlled root fertiliser for larger rooted plants such as:

- Pontederia;
- Iris;
- Alisma;
- Cyperus.

Insert fertiliser below the gravel cap and away from direct contact with the crown.
Begin conservatively and increase only if growth or foliage indicates nutrient deficiency.
The submerged plants should initially receive sufficient nutrients from the shared system without separate fertilisation.

## Routine maintenance

The intended routine should remain relatively simple.

### Regular

- top up evaporated water;
- check pump flow;
- remove dead foliage;
- trim plants that begin shading or smothering neighbours;
- remove obvious algae manually where necessary.

### Periodically

- thin vigorous plants;
- remove excess creeping growth;
- clean the pump intake sponge;
- inspect submerged areas for accumulated decaying material;
- add root fertiliser during active growth where needed.

### Water changes

Routine aquarium-style weekly water changes are not expected to be necessary.
During establishment, or if nutrients/algae accumulate, remove approximately **20–30% of the water** and refill with fresh water.
Once the system stabilises, water changes can be reduced according to actual water clarity, algae growth and plant condition.
Evaporation top-ups will probably be required more often than deliberate water changes.

## Growth control

The collection is deliberately densely planted, so long-term success depends on selective trimming rather than attempting to prevent plants from spreading altogether.

In particular:

- *Oenanthe javanica 'Flamingo'*
    Allow some spreading through the wet area but remove excess runners/growth when it begins competing strongly with smaller plants.
- *Lysimachia nummularia 'Aurea'*
    Use primarily as trailing/creeping foreground growth across stones and the waterline.
    Trim aggressively enough that it does not form a continuous mat over smaller plants.
- *Alisma lanceolatum*
    Remove unwanted flower/seed heads if self-seeding becomes excessive.
- *Cyperus* and *Iris*
    Thin or divide later if their clumps become too large for the available rear planting area.
    Plants can be removed entirely from the collection later if their mature size or growth habit proves unsuitable.

## Seasonal maintenance

Some of the temperate plants may reduce growth or die back substantially during winter.
Do not assume dormant crowns or rhizomes are dead simply because above-water growth disappears.

During winter:

- reduce fertilisation;
- remove dead foliage;
- maintain the water level;
- continue gentle circulation;
- reduce lighting somewhat if plant growth slows strongly;
- keep dormant root systems moist.

Resume feeding and stronger lighting as active growth returns.

## Main operating principles

1. **Keep one shared water reservoir.**
2. **Create different habitats through substrate height and composition.**
3. **Use mineral/inert material for most of the landscape rather than filling everything with nutrient-rich soil.**
4. **Keep submerged soil gravel-capped.**
5. **Maintain gentle circulation rather than strong aquarium flow.**
6. **Keep the system relatively cool.**
7. **Provide strong adjustable artificial lighting.**
8. **Feed rooted plants locally rather than fertilising the whole water column.**
9. **Control the ecosystem mainly by pruning, thinning and removing decaying material.**
10. **Allow the planting to change over time rather than trying to permanently preserve the exact initial arrangement.**
